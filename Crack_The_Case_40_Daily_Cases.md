# Crack The Case — The 40 Daily Mysteries

**Content status:** Rewritten case set for gameplay and editorial review.  
**Difficulty distribution:** 12 Easy · 20 Standard · 8 Challenging.  
**Core design rule:** Every case has exactly one culprit and exactly one decisive clue for the Verdict page. The decisive clue must independently establish the key connection needed to identify the culprit. Other clues provide setting, context, constraints, or rule out alternative explanations; they must not become additional competing answers.

## Content and deduction standards

- Cases use varied deduction mechanisms: physical traces, knowledge only an insider could possess, conflicting accounts, access rules, document interpretation, object provenance, spatial reasoning, impersonation, pattern recognition, motive, and carefully chosen timeline reasoning.
- Chronology is used only when it is genuinely necessary. A case should not become a timestamp puzzle by default.
- Suspect statements should sound like people explaining what they know or did. They should vary in length, content, certainty, and relevance; they must not all be timestamped alibis.
- Clues must not all describe, name, or point directly to the culprit. Most clues should establish neutral facts about the scene, object, process, or competing explanations.
- The culprit may be implicated by one decisive clue. Supporting clues may make the solution understandable, but must not independently identify the culprit.
- The decisive clue must be uniquely named in the answer key and must be the only clue accepted for the correct-clue selection. Do not accept several clues as equivalent.
- A fair solution must follow from the supplied incident, statements, and clues. Do not require outside knowledge, hidden assumptions, or facts not stated in the case.
- The hint should guide the player toward the relevant reasoning operation without naming the culprit or decisive clue.
- The solution explanation must distinguish the decisive clue from contextual clues and explain why the other evidence is not itself decisive.

> **Implementation note:** Preserve each case's facts, suspect identities, clue wording, hint, culprit, decisive clue, and solution explanation when converting this file into structured game content. Do not add alternative decisive clues. Any later editorial revision must preserve a single defensible answer.

## CASE #1 — The Missing Master Key
**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** The master key to the community archive has disappeared from a locked reception drawer. The drawer was not forced, and three staff members knew where the key was kept.

### Suspects
- **Mara Venn — Receptionist:** “I keep the spare drawer key in the ceramic cup. The archive key is normally on the blue hook inside the drawer.”
- **Ivo Grant — Archivist:** “I needed the archive key earlier, but I used my own authorised copy. I have no reason to take the master.”
- **Nell Park — Cleaner:** “I dusted the reception counter. I moved the ceramic cup to wipe underneath it, but I did not open the drawer.”

### Clues
1. **Drawer interior:** The blue hook is empty, but the paper inventory card beside it has not been disturbed.
2. **Ceramic cup:** The cup contains two identical small brass keys. The reception drawer's spare key is the only one with a square green head.
3. **Duplicate key:** A newly cut key is found inside Ivo's locked tool roll. Its teeth match the master key's restricted pattern, and the locksmith's tag identifies it as an unauthorised duplicate.

### Hint
Consider whether the missing object was the master key or whether someone used a different key to access the archive.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Ivo Grant
- **Decisive clue:** Duplicate key
- **Solution explanation:** The unauthorised duplicate in Ivo's locked tool roll matches the restricted master-key pattern. The empty hook confirms the master is missing, while the ceramic cup helps distinguish the drawer's spare key from the archive key. Neither contextual clue identifies who took the master.
- **Deduction check:** Ivo possessed an unauthorised duplicate of the restricted master key, directly connecting him to the removal.

## CASE #2 — The Last Gallery Light
**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** One painting in a small gallery was left in darkness during a public viewing. The lamp itself is intact, and the gallery's lighting circuit is working.

### Suspects
- **Oren Bell — Gallery technician:** “The lamp has its own removable shade. I replaced a cracked shade last week, but the new one was checked before the exhibition.”
- **Tessa Wynn — Curator:** “The painting was visible when I approved the room. I asked for the light to stay on because the frame is difficult to see in shadow.”
- **Cal Doran — Gallery attendant:** “Visitors kept asking whether the painting was part of the evening display. I told them it was, and I did not touch the lamp.”

### Clues
1. **Circuit test:** The lamp socket supplies power normally, and the bulb illuminates when tested outside the gallery.
2. **Display plan:** The painting is the only work in the room whose label specifies a low-angle spotlight.
3. **Shade lining:** The inside of the lamp shade has been coated with opaque black paper. The paper bears a fragment of the gallery's visitor-feedback form, a form printed only at the attendants' desk.

### Hint
The light source works. Look for what could block its light without switching it off.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Cal Doran
- **Decisive clue:** Shade lining
- **Solution explanation:** The black lining deliberately blocked the light. The fragment identifies the paper as coming from the attendants' desk, where Cal worked. The circuit test rules out an electrical failure; the display plan explains why this particular lamp mattered. Neither of those clues identifies who altered the shade.
- **Deduction check:** The shade was deliberately lined with paper originating from Cal's work area. His denial that he touched the lamp is contradicted by the material used to alter it.

## CASE #3 — The Swapped Lunch Labels
**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** Two identical lunch boxes were handed to the wrong conference attendees. One meal was prepared for a guest with a serious allergy, but the mix-up was caught before anyone ate.

### Suspects
- **Rina Cole — Caterer:** “I used the kitchen's colour-coded labels. Red means the allergy-safe meal, and the guest's name is printed beneath the colour.”
- **Dev Shah — Event assistant:** “I arranged the boxes by table number. I did not need to read the dietary notes; the labels were clear.”
- **Milo Reed — Volunteer:** “I carried the boxes from the kitchen. I remember one guest asking whether the red label meant vegetarian.”

### Clues
1. **Dietary sheet:** The guest's allergy-safe meal is correctly recorded as the red-labelled box.
2. **Label adhesive:** Both labels were peeled off cleanly and reapplied; neither box lid was opened.
3. **Label legend:** The printed legend on the label roll has a reversed colour key: it says red means vegetarian, although the kitchen's current system uses red for allergy-safe.

### Hint
Find the point where the meaning of the colour code changed. Then consider who was using the printed reference rather than the kitchen's current system.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Dev Shah
- **Decisive clue:** Label legend
- **Solution explanation:** Dev relied on the outdated printed legend and rearranged the labels according to its reversed key. The dietary sheet establishes which meal needed protection, while the adhesive shows the boxes themselves were not tampered with. Those clues do not identify who used the obsolete key.
- **Deduction check:** The outdated legend explains the exact reversal, and Dev's statement confirms he arranged the boxes using the label system rather than checking the dietary sheet.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** The public-address system in a school auditorium failed during an announcement. The speakers and amplifier are working, but no announcement reached the hall.

### Hint
The speakers work and the microphone route is separate. Trace the recorded player's output and identify the ownership mark on the cable.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Pax Rowan
- **Decisive clue:** Patch cable
- **Solution explanation:** The recorded player's output was diverted using a cable marked with Pax's initials. The speaker test and patch diagram establish how the failure occurred, but do not independently identify who made the change.
- **Deduction check:** Pax's personally labelled test lead was used to divert the announcement output, contradicting his claim that he did not change any connections.

**Incident:** The order of performers for an audition has disappeared from a noticeboard. The schedule was torn, but the signed master copy remains intact in the coordinator's office.

### Suspects

- **Cleo Finch — Assistant:** “I removed the noticeboard's old posters. I did not need the audition order, because the stage manager has a separate running sheet.”

### Clues
1. **Master schedule:** The intact signed copy shows the public order was created by a random draw and not by ranking.
2. **Torn edge:** The missing half was separated along the perforation, not ripped across the writing.
3. **Token pouch:** The numbered audition tokens are still sealed in their draw pouch, but the pouch's seal is a replacement from the assistant's stationery kit.

### Hint
The missing half was not simply torn by accident. Examine how the draw pouch was handled and what the replacement seal tells you.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Cleo Finch
- **Decisive clue:** Token pouch
- **Solution explanation:** The replacement seal from Cleo's kit shows that she opened and resealed the draw pouch. The master schedule establishes that the order came from a draw; the torn edge establishes deliberate removal. Neither alone identifies the person who interfered with the process.
- **Deduction check:** Cleo had access to the assistant's stationery and replaced the draw pouch seal, giving her the opportunity to manipulate the order before the schedule was posted.

## CASE #6 — The False Delivery
**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** A parcel containing replacement parts is marked as delivered, but the receiving office never received it. The courier, receptionist, and office assistant each describe a different part of the handover.

### Suspects
- **Niko Vale — Courier:** “I left the parcel at the building's staffed mailroom. I did not hand it to an individual.”
- **Lena Ford — Receptionist:** “Large parcels go to the mailroom. Reception only signs for letters and small envelopes.”
- **Omar Pike — Office assistant:** “I collected the day's mail. The replacement parts were expected, but I did not see a parcel.”

### Clues
1. **Delivery photograph:** The parcel in the courier's photograph is beside a blue door marked “Returns Depot.”
2. **Building directory:** The intended office's mailroom has a green door and a separate street number.
3. **Parcel label:** The shipping label has the correct destination, but the barcode's final two digits are smudged and unreadable.

### Hint
Compare the location in the delivery photograph with the actual receiving address. Do not assume the label and delivery location match.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Niko Vale
- **Decisive clue:** Delivery photograph
- **Solution explanation:** The parcel was photographed at the returns depot, not at the intended building. The directory confirms that the two locations are different. The smudged barcode could explain a scanning problem, but it does not excuse claiming delivery to the office.
- **Deduction check:** Niko's claim that he left the parcel at the building's mailroom is disproved by the photograph showing it at a different location.

The cabinet was not forced. Look for where the distinctive object went after it left the display.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Uma Bell
- **Decisive clue:** Repair invoice
- **Solution explanation:** The repair invoice connects Uma by name to the distinctive trophy and its transport case. The photograph establishes the unique identifying mark; the intact cabinet shows the trophy was removed without breaking the display. Those clues do not identify who took it.
- **Deduction check:** Uma arranged repair of the trophy with its unique dent and the event's transport case, directly connecting her to the missing object.

## CASE #10 — The Garden Gate
**Difficulty:** Easy

**Incident:** A garden gate was left open and a rare bird escaped its enclosure. The gate is undamaged, and the bird was later found safe in a neighbouring greenhouse.

### Suspects
- **Rafi Noor — Keeper:** “The bird is trained to return to its feeding perch. It rarely flies toward the greenhouse because the glass reflects the trees.”

- **Wes Cole — Night guard:** “I patrol the outer path. I don't enter the enclosure unless the keeper calls me.”

### Clues
1. **Gate mechanism:** The latch can be opened from either side; there is no keypad or electronic release.
2. **Feather trail:** A few feathers on the greenhouse sill match the escaped bird.
3. **Greenhouse reflection panel:** One panel has been turned to face the enclosure. Its polished side creates a clear reflected image of the bird's feeding perch. A small orientation mark on the bracket matches the distinctive mark Tilda uses on the reflective panels she maintains.

### Hint
Consider what might have attracted the bird toward the greenhouse rather than how the gate was opened.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Tilda West
- **Decisive clue:** Greenhouse reflection panel
- **Solution explanation:** The panel was deliberately repositioned to create a familiar-looking reflected perch, luring the bird toward the greenhouse. Its distinctive orientation mark matches Tilda's own maintenance mark. The feather trail confirms the bird's route, while the latch explains that escape did not require a damaged mechanism.
- **Deduction check:** Tilda's distinctive mark appears on the panel deliberately positioned to lure the bird away from its enclosure.

The sleeve shows how the card could be removed without damaging the binder; the typed index explains why the ingredient list alone would not be valuable. Those clues do not identify who took it.
- **Deduction check:** Finn's addressed rubbing connects him to copying the unique original annotations and sending them outside the café.

## CASE #14 — The Clockwork Delay
**Difficulty:** Standard

**Incident:** A museum's clockwork automaton begins its performance with a twelve-minute delay. The gears are intact, and the mechanism has not been damaged.

### Suspects
- **Hugo Trent — Mechanic:** “The automaton uses a counterweight. If the weight is too low, the movement slows, but the clock display remains accurate.”

- **Pavel Orr — Floor manager:** “I moved the display's velvet rope to make room for a wheelchair. The rope stand is kept near the service panel.”

### Clues
1. **Counterweight scale:** The weight is within the manufacturer's operating range.
2. **Performance card:** The printed programme lists the correct start time, so the delay was not part of the schedule.
3. **Gear diagram:** The automaton's timing wheel has a removable brass spacer. The spacer currently installed is stamped with the museum's floor-management inventory mark, a mark used on equipment assigned to Pavel's team.
4. **Case key:** The display case was opened with the shared facilities key; the key register does not record individual users.

### Hint
The mechanism is functioning within its normal weight range. Identify the unusual part and trace which department owns it.

### Authoritative answer key
- **Culprit:** Pavel Orr
- **Decisive clue:** Gear diagram
- **Solution explanation:** The non-standard spacer bears the inventory mark assigned to Pavel's team. The scale rules out the obvious counterweight explanation, while the shared key cannot identify an individual. The ownership mark on the substituted part is the only clue that links the alteration to a suspect.
- **Deduction check:** A part from Pavel's team's inventory was installed in the timing wheel, causing the delay.

## Editorial validation checklist

Before publishing any case, verify all of the following:

1. **One culprit:** Exactly one suspect is responsible based on the supplied information.
2. **One decisive clue:** Exactly one clue is the correct answer for the Verdict page. The other clues must not be accepted as alternative decisive clues.
3. **No suspect pile-on:** The clues are not a stack of separate clues each naming, describing, or directly implicating the same culprit.
4. **Clue diversity:** Clues establish different kinds of information about the incident; they are not merely multiple versions of the same access log, timestamp, or account record.
5. **Statement variety:** Suspect statements are not all alibis or timestamped denials. They reveal relevant knowledge, responsibilities, beliefs, relationships, or explanations in natural language.
6. **Fair inference:** The player can reach the answer from the case content without guessing or relying on unstated facts.
7. **Decisive clue specificity:** The designated clue contains the unique link needed to identify the culprit. If it is removed, the case should no longer have a uniquely defensible culprit.
8. **Supporting clue restraint:** Supporting clues explain the incident, narrow the possibilities, or rule out alternatives without independently proving the culprit.
9. **Hint fairness:** The hint directs attention to the right reasoning task without naming the culprit or giving away the decisive clue.
10. **Solution consistency:** The solution explanation accurately distinguishes the decisive clue from contextual clues and does not claim more than the evidence establishes.
11. **Varied reasoning across the set:** Consecutive cases should not repeat the same deduction mechanism. The full set should include a balanced mix of physical, documentary, spatial, behavioural, knowledge-based, procedural, and timeline reasoning.
12. **No accidental ambiguity:** Names, roles, ownership, access, object descriptions, and clue implications are internally consistent.
